# Mathematicians certify the Riemann hypothesis proof correct, a day after the claim

The Lean and Mathlib communities certified the 17-million-line formal proof on Saturday, while the Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the 1859 problem as unsolved. Researchers have begun a human-readable explanation, but no complete independent exposition of the proof is established.

On Friday, November 20, 2026, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, posted a four-page note on his personal website saying he believed he had, working with the company's Claude AI model, discovered a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. He released the proof not as a paper but as computer code: about 17 million lines written in the Lean proof language, in 14 archive parts. On Saturday, November 21, the Lean and Mathlib communities published a joint statement certifying the proof correct. "We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct, and we congratulate him on this achievement," the statement said. "We hereby certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct."

The Riemann hypothesis was proposed in 1859 by the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann. It asserts that every non-obvious zero of the Riemann zeta function has real part one half, a claim about the hidden pattern behind the distribution of prime numbers. The Clay Mathematics Institute lists it as one of its seven Millennium Prize Problems, with a $1 million prize, and its page still lists the problem as unsolved. The hypothesis has been checked for the first 10 trillion solutions, but a proof for every solution had eluded mathematicians for 167 years.

Alpöge's note says the method also resolves the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, the extension of Riemann's claim to a wider family of functions, and that he expects it to extend further to the Extended Riemann Hypothesis with more time. The verification work checked the generalized claim for Dirichlet L-functions, for every modulus and every character. It did not cover Dedekind zeta functions, Hecke L-functions, or automorphic L-functions in general.

In his note, Alpöge describes months of work with Claude, with a dated timeline running from October 6 to November 16. The decisive idea, as he describes it, descends from Stepanov's argument for the Riemann hypothesis over function fields, adapted to the integers by chaining theta lifts, with the chain length set to the square root of the length of the sum being estimated. He writes that Claude "flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line," and that he expects the argument to be massively simplified, likely immediately. He also says he intends to work to understand the argument and to write it properly. The written version of the full argument would run to more than 250,000 pages, he says, which is why he released only the Lean code.

The joint statement rests its certification on four criteria. First, the statement itself is Mathlib's own definition of the Riemann hypothesis, vetted by experts in Lean and number theory. Second, the library compiles without errors, and dozens of people have now reproduced the compilation. Third, the code proves the correct statement: the community added a file isolating the statement and compared it against the theorem Alpöge proved using two independent tools, comparator and con-leche compare, both confirming the match. Fourth, the exported proof was checked by several proof checkers, including Lean's official kernel and the con-leche and con-ron checkers, plus independent checkers written in other languages. The statement notes that con-leche and con-ron come with a formal proof, relative to set theory, that they cannot accept a false proof.

The fullest public record of the checks is the verification page kept by Andrew Sutherland, a mathematician at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. As of its latest update, at 8:35 p.m. Pacific time on Saturday, all 175,187 modules compile with zero errors, the five headline theorems depend only on the three standard axioms of Lean, and every module has been replayed through the Lean kernel. Two independently written kernels, nanoda and con-leche, accepted the full exported proof, and a third checker, lean4lean, passed every compiled module. An independent rebuild in a sealed sandbox, with no network access, reproduced the exported proof byte for byte. As a cross-check that does not depend on Mathlib's definition of the zeta function, the proof was combined with an independent formalization of Robin's and Lagarias's criteria, producing two theorems about divisor sums with no mention of zeta, and those passed the same checks.

Two additional runs on the full export were still listed as in progress on Sutherland's page at its 8:35 p.m. Pacific update: replay through Lean's default and paranoid kernels and the end-to-end comparator run. Statement comparison and the axiom check had already passed in one comparator run while its kernel stages continued. The MIT page is a dated snapshot that can change as more checks finish. The certification stands on the checks that have finished, and the remaining runs will be added to the record when they report.

The result matters because so much of mathematics has been built on the assumption that Riemann was right. The proof would instantly validate scores of results across mathematics that hinge on the hypothesis being true. "If the proof is correct and understood, this completes a dictionary between different branches of math where the connections were only partial, something like a Rosetta Stone," said Hector Pasten, a mathematician at the Pontifical Catholic University of Chile. "The blast radius extends to the whole of mathematics," said Ghaith Hiary, a mathematician at Ohio State University. "I didn't think I'd see the day, but here we are." James Maynard, a mathematician at Oxford, called it "an absolute historic milestone for mathematics" and "a very new world that we've stepped into."

It also caps a year in which AI systems moved from strength to strength in mathematics. In a September 30, 2026 preprint, OpenAI reported a proof of a zero-free half-plane with real part greater than seven-eighths, a result it called the quasi-Riemann hypothesis. The preprint is a separate mathematical claim, not part of the Alpöge proof. Alpöge's announcement came a day after Anthropic's record-breaking debut on the stock market. That raises questions about the computational resources needed to reproduce or understand enormous AI-assisted proofs, and about who can access the tools used to develop them. The exact research costs are not independently established by this article.

The strangest part of the story is what has not happened yet. A full independent human reconstruction of the 17-million-line argument has not been established. Michael Rubinstein at the University of Waterloo posted a 164-page working guide dated November 21, an important first attempt at an ordinary mathematical account. But that guide explicitly takes a central inequality from the Lean development rather than proving it independently. Alpöge himself writes that he still intends to master the surrounding mathematics and explain the method properly. "The point of math, and the point of science, is understanding why things happen, not just collecting statements of truth," said Maksym Radziwill, a mathematician at New York University. The formal checks provide powerful evidence that the code proves the mathematical statements claimed. Understanding the underlying argument, simplifying it, and meeting the separate standards of the wider mathematical community remain work ahead.

## What to watch next

The two pending runs on Sutherland's verification page come first: the Lean kernel replay of the full export and the end-to-end comparator run. Their results will close the last open items in the verification record.

The second watch is the human one. Alpöge has said he intends to understand the argument, simplify it, and write it properly, and he expects shorter arguments to follow. Rubinstein's 164-page working draft is already an initial reading guide, but it relies on the Lean proof for a crucial inequality. Mathematicians will be looking for a complete, independently understood exposition and for simplifications that show which parts of the 17-million-line development were essential.

The third watch is the Clay prize. The institute's rules require a proposed solution to be published in a qualifying outlet, at least two years to pass after that publication, and the solution to achieve general acceptance in the global mathematics community. The problem is still listed as unsolved, and the prize is years away even if the certification holds.

A fourth watch arrived the same day as the claim. Alessandro Masullo, an independent mathematician based in Rome, and Brian Conrad of Stanford University announced a draft proof developed without AI. Masullo had spent nearly 12 years on the work and kept it under wraps for fear of being scooped, while Conrad reviewed it privately for months. The plan was to spend another year addressing errors in the 940-page manuscript and circulating it among experts. The AI announcement forced their hand, and they released the unfinished manuscript before those issues were resolved. Whether that proof holds up remains to be seen, and an earlier attempt by Masullo failed to withstand scrutiny. Alpöge also released advances on the Euler and Navier-Stokes equations the same day, a separate thread worth following on its own.

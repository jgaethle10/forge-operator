# Yakima's Camera Debate Is About More Than Halloween
*Evercraft Journal | Local affairs, technology and public accountability | Working draft, October 31, 2026*
*Editorial state: evidence-gated, unpublished. Four substantive story paragraphs below. Verify meeting packet, trailer operator, and right of reply before approval.*

**Dek:** Mobile surveillance towers appeared in a Yakima neighborhood conversation just as a dispute over permanent live-feed cameras approached a council decision. The central question is not whether technology can help police. It is what rules govern the technology, who can verify them, and who gets to decide.

## 1. The Halloween sighting
A Halloween neighborhood post in Yakima circulated photographs of trailer-mounted camera towers and warned trick-or-treating families about equipment seen on residential streets. The photographs establish that the posted images depict mobile surveillance equipment; they do **not** independently establish who owns it, which system it uses, whether it records license plates, or why it was deployed. That distinction matters amid a highly charged local debate. Yakima Police Chief Shawn Boyle had publicly acknowledged talk of a Halloween “National De-Flock Day” targeting surveillance cameras and urged lawful protest rather than vandalism, following reports of damaged equipment. The sightings are a useful reporting lead, not proof of a retaliatory deployment. Before making that claim, the responsible agencies should be asked for deployment authorizations, vendor information, operating policies, costs, and records documenting what was collected. [Source: NonStop Local on vandalism and police comments](https://www.nbcrightnow.com/yakima/2-more-yakima-flock-cameras-cut-down-3-total-in-the-last-2-months/article_15e3fc0e-eb6f-448f-bc03-380a0869befe.html).

## 2. Two technologies, one expanding debate
The dispute is bigger than two trailers. Yakima Police has operated a network of fixed Flock automatic license plate readers, while Yakima County Sheriff Robert Udell described a separate two-year, **$2 million** pilot to install **150 live-feed cameras** countywide; at the time of his September 10 remarks, he said **50 to 70** were already in place. Udell argued that camera placement follows crime patterns and that video search tools can help investigators find relevant objects. Yakima Police has similarly argued that technology helps cover staffing shortages. Critics, including community advocates and local officials, question whether collection practices, location choices, access and oversight have been adequately explained. The distinction between a camera that converts plates into searchable vehicle records and a system supplying searchable live video is crucial: a policy describing one does not automatically answer what the other records or retains. [Source: county pilot reporting](https://www.nbcrightnow.com/yakima/150-live-feed-flock-cameras-coming-to-yakima-county-at-2m-cost/article_d6305813-e4a5-48c6-957f-6ea4c9ddfd8f.html) · [Source: October council debate](https://www.nbcrightnow.com/yakima/yakima-council-to-vote-on-removing-live-feed-flock-cameras/article_6cba56c4-bd91-40b0-8ad1-108b208877ef.html).

## 3. What the law and public dashboard actually say
Washington's 2026 law, codified as **RCW 10.130**, restricts government automated license plate readers, regulates permitted purposes and sharing, requires registration and audits, and generally limits data retention to **21 days**, with statutory exceptions. Crucially, its definition turns on technology that converts license-plate images into computer-readable data; the law is not a blanket set of protections for every live-video system. Yakima Police's Flock transparency portal, **last updated July 24, 2026**, listed **367,279 unique plate reads** and **495 user search sessions** in its displayed 30-day period. Those are historical portal figures, not October 31 totals, individual people tracked, or proof of improper searches. The portal also describes acceptable use and access restrictions, including prohibitions on immigration enforcement and personal use. Those publicly stated restrictions are meaningful, but meaningful oversight also requires records showing how they are tested, how violations are handled and whether live-feed deployments follow equivalent rules. [Source: Washington statute](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=10.130&full=true) · [Source: Yakima Police Flock portal](https://transparency.flocksafety.com/yakima-wa-pd).

## 4. A decision, and the questions it should answer
The issue is reaching a decision point. Local reporting says Yakima City Council scheduled consideration of a resolution on **Monday, November 2, 2026**, concerning county-owned permanent live-feed cameras within city limits, while preserving a proposed exception for mobile cameras at permitted public events. The city's own calendar also lists a council meeting that evening. Deputy Mayor Reedy Berg has raised concerns about waiting for legislative action without clear safeguards; Mayor Matt Brown has questioned location transparency and the effect on privacy. Police leaders point to public-safety uses and diminished staffing. Those are competing claims that can be evaluated through evidence rather than assumed motives. Before any vote, residents deserve a public account of the contracts, locations and siting criteria, data categories collected, retention and deletion schedules, access and interagency sharing permissions, independent audits, documented investigative results, and procedures for complaints. Violence or vandalism should not determine surveillance policy, and surveillance policy should not escape scrutiny because vandalism occurred.

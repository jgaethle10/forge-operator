# Nebraska | The Giant Stirs: Real-Person Interview Field Guide

**Status: production preparation only.** As of October 21, 2026 no fan, former player, coach, historian or journalist has agreed to participate and no human interview has been recorded. This document does not confer access, consent, or a legal right to publish another person's voice/image.

## Find the subjects who might change the story

**Longtime supporter:** Someone whose lived relationship to the team spans more than one coaching era and who can give concrete memories from a game, household, radio call or workday. Do not assume their stories are about championship nostalgia or require them to compare eras favorably.

**Younger supporter:** Someone with a meaningfully different entry point, perhaps their first trip to Lincoln, a family ritual, or memories of more difficult seasons. Let them disagree without making them the “impatient youth” stereotype.

**Contextual voice:** An independent historian, regional reporter, former player or football researcher who can distinguish what actually happened from myths and give the Indiana/Oregon games factual context. Identify affiliation and potential conflicts. Access to someone employed by the team is an open question, not promised.

Aim for at least one personally motivated, usable scene from both fans and an identifiable independent analysis voice before treating Episode 1 as assembly-ready. Their names, contact details, releases, dates, and raw recordings are absent from the registry until genuinely acquired. Never infer consent from a public comment or a social-media clip.

## Conversational discovery, not scripted testimony

After introductions and an informed explanation of the project, invite the participant to speak in their own terms. Begin with something concrete and sensory: “What's the first Nebraska football moment you can actually picture?” “Who was there?” “What were you doing before the game?” “Is there a place in your life where Nebraska football has always turned up?” Follow the person's answer rather than immediately asking a question that supports the preferred arc.

Explore change across time without presupposing optimism: “Has what you want from this team changed?” “What did you hope the 2026 team could do before Indiana?” “Which moment of that game stuck with you, if you watched it?” “Did Oregon change your impression or confirm it?” “What would count as genuine progress to you?” “What would an outsider misunderstand about caring this much?” “What do you *not* want the filmmakers to get wrong?”

When revisiting the person after the next games, use open follow-ups: “What surprised you?” “What do you remember differently now?” “Do you still agree with what you said in our first conversation?” Leave room for changed minds. Do not insert sample answers, ask participants to reenact disappointment, or claim they witnessed games they did not see.

## Capture one honest scene rather than generic B-roll

Get permission for a quiet location that matters personally to the subject: a living room, workbench, community venue, radio room or walk toward a stadium. Don't frame an unrelated workshop as a participant's job or a staged empty stadium as present-day access. Record the person's face, hands and surroundings without manufacturing activity.

Capture long responses, neutral listening reaction, and 30 seconds of room tone on the actual microphone. Keep camera and separate audio clocks synchronized if available, check peak levels before each session, monitor clipping and wind, record actual sync slates and the original unprocessed interview. An individual's own home/workplace footage also requires location consent where applicable. Seek safe, accessible participation and do not pressure volunteers who decline.

Minimum internal source receipt fields: participant's real preferred credit, contact method held privately, session date/time/timezone, location and venue permission if applicable, crew/operator, recorder/camera models when known, raw filenames, cryptographic hashes, on-camera consent and any signed releases, third-party music/TV/noisy-room restrictions, associated transcript timestamps, editing restrictions and follow-up owner. Keep personal contact details out of the public GitHub repository.

## Permissions are separate from filming

Before capture, describe the film's working title, independent nature, possible platforms (including promotional excerpts if contemplated), intended uses of video/audio, voluntary participation, compensation if any, ability to decline a question, and how to reach the production contact. For published/commercial distribution, obtain the appropriate signed participant release reviewed for the applicable jurisdiction. Consent to speak is not automatically permission to use a voice for AI synthesis, voice cloning, ads unrelated to the film, or promotional endorsements.

Do not obtain or store private signatures, addresses, phone numbers or raw participant identities in open GitHub issues. Only non-sensitive receipt identifiers and rights state belong in public project manifests. If a participant withdraws or requests an agreed restriction, reconcile those terms before distributing any cut.

## Interview scene admission gate

An interview is not ready for an episode until the raw recording actually exists, source hash and session identity are verified, the real participant is not being impersonated, relevant releases/permissions are documented, audio/visual quality has been auditioned, the edit preserves meaning and context, and a human director accepts a truthful scene. A prompt describing an ideal quote remains a **production need**, not a recorded human voice. Do not synthesize a quote into the film and call it archival testimony.

## Suggested first filming objective

Produce one **five-minute candidate sequence** featuring a long-time supporter, an early source-verified football shot and a younger supporter whose answer complicates the first person's view. The sound should be the actual interview, with any musical treatment separately earned and licensed. Before expanding to Episode 1's full 25 minutes, ask a viewer who is not already a Huskers fan to describe whom they cared about and what question makes them want to watch Episode 2. If that test fails, capture better reality instead of adding editing flourishes.

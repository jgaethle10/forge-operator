# Evercraft Fungal Defense | Claim review

**Working title:** The hospital fungus was not discovered this week  
**Research ID:** FUNGAL-DEFENSE-001  
**Prepared:** November 15, 2026  
**Editorial state:** Source-reviewed internal draft, **not published**  
**Review standard:** Journal-first, original sources, no fear framing

## The central finding

Posts describing a drug-resistant fungus in hospitals as if it were newly discovered in November 2026 can be misleading if they refer to *Candida auris* (also known as *Candidozyma auris*).

The fungus was first described in 2009, after identification from an external-ear specimen in Japan. CDC issued a U.S. healthcare alert in **June 2016**, and publicly described the first reported U.S. cases in **November 2016**. Resistance and healthcare-associated transmission were known concerns at that time.

There *is* a new development: on **November 13, 2026**, CDC updated its annual U.S. clinical-case tracking table through **2025**. The latest table reports **8,780 clinical cases in 2025**, compared with a **revised 6,370 in 2024**. Earlier versions of the same CDC tracking series reported **6,304 for 2024**. The revision is material and should be identified and dated, not silently merged.

An increase in reported clinical cases is important. It is **not** proof that a new fungus appeared in a hospital this week, that all antifungal drugs have stopped working, or that all patients are at equal risk.

## Evidence ledger

| Claim | Classification | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Candida auris was just discovered in 2026" | False | CDC documented it in a June 2016 alert and a November 2016 report; first described in 2009 |
| "C. auris can resist antifungal drugs" | Established | CDC clinical guidance describes frequent resistance, increasing echinocandin resistance and rare pan-resistance |
| "Every medicine is useless against C. auris" | False | CDC continues to recommend echinocandins as initial treatment for many clinical infections; professional susceptibility-based care is essential |
| "Hospitals face a real infection-control challenge" | Established | CDC documents transmission among vulnerable patients in healthcare facilities |
| "The number 8,780 means 8,780 deaths or invasive infections" | False | The CDC table is **reported clinical cases**, not a death count |
| "There was a new outbreak at a specific hospital this week" | Unverified without a named hospital and dated health-authority report | A general Facebook post cannot establish a specific local outbreak |
| "The 2025 annual total proves a 2026 pandemic is inevitable" | Unsupported | No inference about 2026 outcomes or pandemic probability follows from these retrospective counts |

## What actually helps

CDC emphasizes timely recognition, appropriate patient screening, infection prevention and control, and effective healthcare environmental cleaning. Treatment decisions belong to qualified medical professionals, not this research project. Colonization alone is not the same as clinical infection and does not by itself call for antifungal treatment.

## Evercraft evidence policy

- The **organism's discovery date** must never be confused with a **publication date** or a **new case-data snapshot**.
- Link to original CDC evidence and label the date, surveillance scope and case definition.
- Preserve 2024's old 6,304 case snapshot alongside the revised 6,370 figure.
- Keep final annual 2025 reports distinct from provisional 2026 weekly NNDSS notifications.
- For social claim triage, ask what fungus, where, when, who reported it, and whether the claim is new to the public or new to science.
- Do not infer personal medical risk, identify patients/facilities from suppressed data, run pathogen experiments or publish outbreak alerts.
- **Publication and automated distribution remain blocked** pending Systemia/owned Journal route and human evidence review. This markdown is not a Journal publication receipt.

**Editorial angle:** The newsworthiness is not a newly discovered fungus. It is the scale, growth and resistance challenges of a known pathogen, plus the fact that recycled social posts can blur crucial scientific timelines.
